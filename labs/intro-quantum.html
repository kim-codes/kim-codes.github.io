First, Some Context

How much of what we've heard about quantum computing is true?

Quantum computers are just more powerful computers. Myth or Reality? Reveal the answer click here

Quantum computers work differently in how they process information compared to classical computers. They use the principles of quantum mechanics which have advantages for particular problems. For example, simulating molecular interactions with high precision, or factoring large numbers for cryptography.

They are not a replacement for classical computers.

Quantum computers will replace classical computers. Myth or Reality? Reveal the answer

As we've discussed, and as we'll see shortly, quantum computers work differently from classical computers. That difference could help solve certain problems, but it doesn't make them faster or better at everything. So don't expect them to replace your laptop.

"I thought you needed special hardware to experiment with quantum computing." Myth or Reality? Reveal the answer

You can build and run quantum circuits on a regular computer using a simulator. And that's exactly what we're going to do!

There's still a lot to figure out with quantum computing. The hardware is improving, researchers are experimenting, and we're a long way from using quantum computers for everyday tasks.

Luckily, we don't need one sitting on our desk to start experimenting.

Let's take a look at bits and logic gates.

The familiar

How do computers process information?

Everything your computer does, from displaying a photo to running a game, behind the scenes it's all 1s and 0s.

We call those 1s and 0s bits. Each bit is either a 0 or 1, and computers combine billions of them to represent information.

In order for your computer to work, they need to use logic gates to perform actions on those bits. Logic gates are simple operations that follow rules, like flipping a 0 to a 1.

A little logic

Now that we've turned our letter A into bits, let's do something with them.

Remember those logic gates? One of the simplest is the NOT gate. Its job is to flip a bit: a 0 becomes a 1, and a 1 becomes a 0.

Try clicking the highlighted bit in our letter A. What happens to the letter when you change just one bit?

The unfamiliar

Meet the qubit

Quantum computers don't use bits. They use qubits. Unlike a bit, a qubit can exist in a superposition of 0 and 1. Think of it as a combination of the two possible states, rather than a definite 0 or 1.

When we check the value of a qubit (when we 'measure' it), we still get either a 0 or a 1. But what is interesting is what we can do with the qubit before we measure it.

A bit vs. a qubit

Qubit Can be in a superposition

Since quantum computers use qubits, we can't use logic gates. We use what we call quantum gates in order to process information.

One of these is the Hadamard gate (H). When applied to a qubit starting in the state |0⟩, it creates an equal superposition of 0 and 1.

If we measure that qubit, there's a 50% chance of getting 0 and a 50% chance of getting 1.

And that's the basics.
